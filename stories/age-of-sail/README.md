# The Drowned Hours

*An Age of Sail story campaign for Elsewhere: ten chapters and a frame world, about five and a half hours of play.*

## Logline

Havana, July 1715. Fished out of the harbour with no name, no papers and no flag, you sail as a cosmographer's apprentice with the Spanish treasure fleet and its most closely guarded cargo, a sealed casket registered as "rubies for the Queen". When a hurricane drowns the fleet on the Florida coast, the casket floats away. The race to find it carries you through a salvage camp, a pirate raid and the beaches of Nassau to an island no two charts agree on. What's inside isn't jewels. It's worth more than all the fleet's silver, and at the end you decide who owns it.

## Tone and themes

**Tone.** A salt-and-sunlight adventure with real weather and real history: funny in port, frightening at sea, and honest about what the silver cost. The heroes love instruments, maps and people more than gold. Violence is brief and never graphic: a beaten fighter yields or goes over the rail with a splash. The dead of 1715 are mourned, not shown. The rating target is T.

**Themes.**
1. **Who owns what the sea gives back?** Admiralty law sorts wreckage into flotsam, jetsam, lagan and derelict, and argues over who keeps each kind. The player is named Flotsam. The casket is flotsam too. A crown, a syndicate, the pirates, the salvors and a grieving inventor all claim it.
2. **Knowing where you are.** In 1715 any navigator can find latitude, but nobody can find longitude at sea, and ships die of the difference. The player's arc runs from Flotsam, adrift, to Pilot, someone who knows the position and steers others by it.
3. **Records and remembrance.** In this world, ledgers enslave people and free them, hide stolen silver and name the drowned. Keeping an honest record takes courage. That suits a platform where every visit is a verified record.
4. **Small mercies.** Fenwick's ship is the *Small Mercy*, and the story keeps paying them back: a line thrown to an enemy, a promise kept, a true light lit for everyone, enemies included.

## The player character: Flotsam

**Who.** Whoever walks out of Junction. The campaign never fixes the avatar's body, sex, age or origin, and no line assumes any of them. In 1715 the player is a stranger pulled out of Havana harbour with nothing: no papers, no flag, no past anyone can check. Lalo, the twelve-year-old who hauls them into his skiff, names them **Flotsam**, and the name sticks. Other characters have their own names for the player. Fenwick says "Counsel", Rufina comes to say "Forty", Wraxall says "my friend from nowhere" and Tamsin says "my bird". The player speaks only through dialogue choices, which are written in the player's voice.

**Why they're there.** Inés Abarca needs a pair of hands that belong to no king. A stranger with no country can't be bribed by a flag, claimed by a crown or accused of spying for anyone. That's also what makes Flotsam useful to everyone else: in a world carved up by treaties, a person without papers can walk into every faction's camp.

**How they reach the story from Junction.**
1. Junction shows one new portal: **THE DROWNED HOURS**, subtitled *Havana, 1715*. It leads to the **Chart Room** (world `dh-chartroom`), never straight into a chapter. Chapter worlds are hidden from Junction's automatic portal ring (BUILD.md, feature 1).
2. The Chart Room is a cartographer's study in Havana in 1760. Don Lázaro Quintana, aged 57, is drawing at a chart table. He is Lalo grown up, and he recognises the player although they have never met: "You don't know me yet. I was twelve, and I was about to fish you out of the harbour." A great framed chart of the Caribbean hangs on the north wall, and its glass is water. Walking into it starts Chapter 1.
3. From then on the Chart Room is the campaign's hub. It holds the chapter select (finished chapters are pins on the chart table), the codex (Don Lázaro's notebooks), the memorial (the Book of the Drowned) and a case of rewards. Its furnishings change with the ending the player reaches.
4. Each chapter ends by travelling to the next (`w.travel`). Leaving mid-chapter returns the player to the Chart Room, and the chapter resumes from its last beacon.
5. **Replays.** At the chart table the player can choose **Remember it as it was**, which replays a chapter with every choice locked to the canon answer while collectibles still count. **Tell it differently** rewinds the canon instead: it clears every flag set in that chapter and the chapters after it, and the story continues from there. An ending can be chosen again by retelling Chapter 10 alone.

## Setting and history

**The world in 1715.** The Peace of Utrecht (1713) has ended the War of the Spanish Succession. Thousands of English, French and Dutch privateers found their commissions void overnight, and the line between privateer and pirate became a single signature. Spain hasn't been able to send its silver fleets home for years, so a huge backlog of silver, gold, Chinese porcelain and cochineal waits in Havana. The crown is drowning in war debt and Philip V has just married Elisabeth Farnese, so Madrid wants that cargo now. At Utrecht, Britain won the **Asiento**: for thirty years it may sell enslaved Africans in Spanish America, 4,800 people a year, so English "permission ships" now dock in Havana. In July 1714 the British Parliament passed the **Longitude Act**, offering up to £20,000 to anyone who can find longitude at sea. Spain's own prize, offered by Philip III in 1598, has never been won. The 1707 wreck of Sir Cloudesley Shovell's fleet on the Isles of Scilly, lost because its navigators didn't know where they were, still haunts every chart room.

**Folklore used.** The Giraldilla weathervane of Havana and the legend of Inés de Bobadilla watching the sea for a husband who never came back. The Lusca of the Bahamian blue holes. Phantom islands such as Antillia, which gave the Antilles their name, and Bermeja. The Taíno word *hurakán*.

**Liberties taken.**
- Inés, her sea clock *La Golondrina* and its casket are invented. No working marine timekeeper existed in 1715; John Harrison's H1 came twenty years later. The clock's twin counter-swinging balances anticipate his idea, and the codex says so and credits him.
- The Wandering Isle, Tamsin's lighthouse and the cay it stands on are invented. The Matanilla shoals at the north end of the Little Bahama Bank are real and notorious.
- Inés's casket aboard Ubilla's flagship, *Nuestra Señora de Regla*, is fiction, and so is every conversation with a real person.
- Self-purchase was a Spanish custom from the 16th century. The formal contract the story uses, with a fixed price, instalments and a notary's record, became standard in Cuba later in the 18th century; we use it a few decades early.
- Sources disagree on the fleet's departure date and the raid's date. We use 24 July 1715 and the first week of January 1716.
- The *Griffon* survives in every version of the story. Warning its captain changes only a letter in the epilogue.

**Handling the era's cruelties.**
- Slavery is in the story because it was everywhere: in the mines that filled the fleet, in the Asiento, among the salvage divers, on the sugar estate Sunday fled and on the beach at Nassau where captives were sold. The story names it plainly and never makes it scenery, a mini-game or a reward.
- Enslaved and formerly enslaved characters (Rufina, Tito, Sunday) have names, histories, goals and humour, and they drive their own plots. The player can help or hinder but never frees anyone by grace: Tito's freedom comes from his own contract, his own flight or his sister's wages.
- No violence against enslaved people is shown. Merino's threats are spoken aloud and answered.
- Narration and codex say "enslaved person". Period characters may say "slave" where history demands it. Nobody ever uses a slur.
- Indigenous peoples are acknowledged honestly in the codex (the Ais of the wreck coast, the Lucayans of the Bahamas, the Taíno origin of "hurricane"). None of them is turned into a mystical guide.
- Pirates aren't heroes. Some crews voted, shared fairly and took in escaped men, and the same beaches sold captives. Sunday says so out loud.

## Factions

- **The Crown and the Flota (Spain).** Ubilla, Salmón and Anselmo. Spain owns the fleet and the silver and, it believes, the rubies. It wants the King's property back, the dead counted and the salvage protected. It treats the player as a useful foreigner, and as a friend of the King if the player earns that.
- **The Flying Gang (Nassau).** Hornigold's loose brotherhood of former privateers, with Jennings as a Jamaican wolf on its edge and Fenwick's *Small Mercy* among its sloops. They want Spanish silver, freedom from every king, and to avoid hanging. They run on articles and votes.
- **The Restitution (Wraxall's syndicate).** A ring of London backers and the hired crew of the sloop *Restitution*. The backers want the sea clock to win the British prize. The crew wants pay and to stay alive.
- **The Divers.** A community rather than a faction: the free and enslaved divers of the salvage camp, who know the wrecks better than anyone and whose names no ledger spells right. They want wages, safety, freedom and their names written down.
- **The Keeper.** Tamsin and her unfinished light on the Wandering Isle. She wants the light lit, the isle on the charts and no more wrecks.

## Cast

Sixteen speaking roles. Five are real people (marked †), portrayed from what the record says about them.

1. **Flotsam** (the player). A stranger out of Havana harbour with no flag and no papers. Silent except through choices, which run from earnest to wry. **Arc:** from Flotsam, adrift and claimed by whoever finds them, to Pilot, who chooses a course for others. Titles mark the way.

2. **Inés Abarca, *la Cosmógrafa*** (54). An instrument- and chart-maker from Seville, daughter of the late Cosmographer of the Casa de Contratación; the post was refused to her because she is a woman. She is a widow, and her son Mateo, a ship's boy, drowned in 1705 when his ship misjudged its longitude and struck Los Mártires, the Florida Keys. She spent ten years building *La Golondrina*, the Swallow, a sea clock with twin balances and twelve ruby bearings, to win Spain's longitude prize and her father's title. To hide it from spies she registered it as rubies for the Queen, and so made it the most hunted object in the Caribbean. **Voice:** dry, exact, quick to scold and quicker to praise exact work; she talks in measurements. "Measure twice. Pray once." She says "be so good as to" instead of "please". **Arc:** from wanting her own name on the hour to wanting the hour on every ship, or to whatever end the player chooses. Havana gossips call her "the second Inés who watches the sea", after the Giraldilla.

3. **Lázaro "Lalo" Quintana** (12, and Don Lázaro, 57, in the 1760 frame). A Havana dock boy and Inés's errand runner, who sells hand-drawn maps of places he's never been ("here, a whale the size of Seville"). He fishes Flotsam out of the harbour and names them, then stows away on the flagship. **Voice:** rapid, boastful and sweet, with every number enormous. As an old man he's wry and gentle and honest about the boy he was. **Arc:** from drawing what he wishes were there to drawing what is there. By 1760 he is Havana's chart-maker and the frame's narrator.

4. **Captain Absalom Fenwick** (44). A Bristol attorney's clerk turned privateer, whose Jamaican commission died with the Peace of Utrecht: "They made me a pirate with a quill." Elected captain of the sloop *Small Mercy*, he's caught smuggling in Havana. **Voice:** florid legal English full of "whereas" and "notwithstanding", mock-solemn and secretly soft; he calls the player "Counsel" and counts votes in his sleep. **Arc:** if freed, from self-interest to loyalty. If abandoned, a businessman. If betrayed, a man whose crew outvotes his grudge.

5. **Quartermaster Kwesi "Sunday" Mensah** (35). The *Small Mercy*'s elected quartermaster. He was born in Jamaica to Akan parents (Kwesi means Sunday-born) and walked off a sugar estate at fifteen. He is free by his own act and his crew's articles, and he holds the crew's vote, the ship's purse and its conscience. **Voice:** few words, low and deliberate. He reads the articles like scripture, and his humour is very dry. **Arc:** he tests whether Flotsam is fair, and he decides whether the crew follows them at the end. He's also the one who names Nassau's hypocrisy aloud.

6. **Jasper Wraxall** (46), the antagonist. In 1707 he was a master's mate in Shovell's fleet. He told his captain they were further east than their reckoning, and the captain laughed. He survived the night on a rock, and whispers blamed him for the loss. Now a London syndicate backs him in the race for the 1714 prize, and he sails the sloop *Restitution*. **Voice:** soft and courteous; he corrects bearings mid-sentence and never raises his voice. "Let us be exact." **Arc:** a mirror of Inés, with the same wound and the opposite cure. He wants the hour to belong to whoever paid for it, and mercy can still reach him.

7. **Rufina Ocampo** (32). A salvage diver, born enslaved on the pearl banks of Riohacha, who bought her own freedom with pearls dived on her own time. She now dives for wages and holds her breath longer than anyone on the coast. **Voice:** brief and dry. She counts in breaths ("that's a thirty-breath swim") and never lets anyone write her name wrong. **Arc:** she wants to buy her brother Tito's freedom, and she trusts slowly. She leads her own plan in Chapter 6. Her ending depends on the player's choices, but never on the player rescuing her.

8. **Faustino "Tito" Ocampo** (17). Rufina's younger brother, an enslaved diver owned by the salvage contractor Merino. He talks underwater by knocking on hulls and wants to be a ship's carpenter. Teasing, brave, impatient. **Arc:** freedom by his own contract, by flight, or by a long wait, depending on Chapters 3 and 6.

9. **Don Anselmo Cuevas** (60). The fleet's *escribano* (notary) aboard the flagship. He keeps the registro and his own protocol book of contracts, he's terrified of water, and there's ink on his cuffs. **Voice:** formal, fussy and kind; he addresses the player as "honoured Flotsam". **Arc:** from timid clerk to keeper of the Book of the Drowned, a list of a thousand names, and the one man who refuses to falsify the salvage record.

10. **Tamsin Penhallow** (63). A Cornish widow. Her husband Jory was a shipwright on Rudyard's Eddystone tower, and a Bermudian merchant hired them both to build a light on the Matanilla shoals. The merchant went bankrupt and Jory died in 1713, but Tamsin stayed to finish the light alone. After the storm a ticking box washed up on her rocks, and she has wound it every morning since. She calls it "the Heart". **Voice:** a Cornish lilt ("dreckly", "proper job", "my bird"). She sings to her lamp and is exactly as superstitious as she is practical. **Arc:** from guarding a dead man's unfinished tower to lighting it, or watching it go dark.

11. **Admiral Don Francisco Salmón**†. Commander of the survivors' and salvage camp at Palmar de Ais, who surrendered to Jennings in 1716. **Voice:** clipped and tired, scrupulous with numbers. He cares more for his survivors than he lets on.

12. **Captain-General Juan Esteban de Ubilla**†. Commander of the Nueva España fleet. He sails late in the hurricane season under pressure from the crown and dies in the wreck. **Voice:** grave, courteous, burdened: a man with a king's letter in his pocket.

13. **Captain Antoine Daire**†. Master of the French *Griffon*, the fleet's only survivor, which stays further offshore. **Voice:** cheerful and sceptical. He trusts his nose more than any glass.

14. **Captain Henry Jennings**†. A Jamaican planter turned privateer whose commission "against pirates", signed by Governor Hamilton, becomes his licence to raid the Spanish salvage camp. **Voice:** hard and plain, and legalistic about his paper.

15. **Captain Benjamin Hornigold**†. Senior captain of Nassau's Flying Gang, who won't take an English prize. **Voice:** gruff, paternal, amused by the young.

16. **Elias Oakes** (58). Wraxall's old sailing master, a decent Whitby seaman in bad company, who owes the player his life if they throw him a line in Chapter 8. **Voice:** plain Yorkshire, short sentences.

**Minor roles:** Don Gaspar Merino, the salvage contractor who owns Tito; Bosun Ochoa and Old Tomé the caulker of the flagship; Benito Ruiz, a gunner's mate stranded on the *Urca*; Hob, a young topman; Don Bernabé, Havana's harbourmaster; the *Small Mercy*'s bosun Ezra Pike and cook Pease; Uncle Asa, an old Eleuthera fisherman who tells the tale of the Lusca; and soldiers, sailors and market sellers with a line or two each.

## The story

### Act I: The Hour Before (Havana and the fleet, 22–29 July 1715)

**1. Noon at Havana** (`dh-01-havana`). The player comes to in Lalo's skiff, is named Flotsam, and crosses the waterfront past a polite Englishman (Wraxall) and a caged privateer (Fenwick) who begs for help. Inés lets no one in by the door, so Lalo leads Flotsam across the rooftops to reach her Sun Loft before the noon bell. Under the loft's meridian line Flotsam takes their first noon sight, works out Havana's latitude and is hired. At dusk soldiers carry the casket of the Queen's Rubies to the flagship. Anselmo writes it down as twenty-three pounds, to be kept upright, and Inés whispers an order: wind the Queen every noon. That night Wraxall offers money for information, and Fenwick's fate is in the player's hands. Freeing him means walking the La Punta capstan to slacken the harbour chain under his boat.

**2. The Silver Road** (`dh-02-flota`). Aboard *Nuestra Señora de Regla* with eleven sail around her. Lalo turns up stowed away in the cacao sacks and becomes cabin boy. Flotsam learns to heave the log and box the compass, and every noon slips outside the stern galleries, above the sea, to wind the casket through a sealed port. The storm signs pile up: a ground swell out of the south-east, mares' tails, birds flying for land and Inés's falling weather-glass. Ubilla listens and sails on anyway, with a king's letter in his pocket. From the maintop at sunset the whole fleet lies under a copper sky. On the last night Inés tells Flotsam about her drowned son and asks for a promise.

### Act II: The Drowned Hours (the hurricane and the wreck coast, 30 July 1715 – January 1716)

**3. Hurakán** (`dh-03-hurakan`). The hurricane, played on deck. Flotsam chocks a loose cannon, cuts a fouled topsail in the gusts, crosses the fallen mainmast and pulls Lalo off the bowsprit. In the stern cabin Inés straps the casket into its cork jacket and gives Flotsam her pocket watch, which keeps Havana time. The window bursts, and Flotsam can save only one book: Inés's journal or Anselmo's protocol book. Inés pushes the casket out of the window and is swept after it. The ship strikes, and Flotsam glides from the breaking stern on a torn topsail, lands on a hatch cover and rides the surf to shore by lightning.

**4. The Silver Tide** (`dh-04-coast`). Dawn on a beach of wreckage and survivors. Flotsam finds water, the scattered belongings of the dead, Inés's hat and a torn strap from the casket's cork jacket, which shows it floated free. At low water Flotsam carries a line to survivors stranded on the *Urca*'s sterncastle and races the flood back. A gunner's mate there saw the box riding north on the current, and saw an English boat pull a woman off a hatch cover. Two weeks later Salmón's relief ships arrive. Flotsam must choose whether to tell him the truth, and meets Rufina, who recognises the drilled rubies as watch bearings. Plotting the drift points to the Matanilla shoals, forty leagues of reef. To narrow it down Flotsam needs Inés's own chart, which lies in the wreck.

**5. Forty Breaths** (`dh-05-wreck`). Rufina teaches free-diving: forty breaths, air pockets, and Tito's knock code. Inside the flagship's broken stern Flotsam finds the casket's torn cradle and Inés's chart case, then escapes as a swell rolls the hull. That night, by the fire, comes the midpoint reveal. Inés's letters show that the Queen's Rubies is a longitude clock and the rubies are its bearings. Her chart gives the Wandering Isle's true position in a compass-point cipher. Tito is trapped in a collapse during a dive for Merino, and Flotsam frees him. Merino makes threats, and raises Tito's price.

**6. The Raid at Palmar** (`dh-06-palmar`). Jennings's three sloops, one of them the *Small Mercy*, arrive at dusk. Everyone wants something from Flotsam. Salmón wants a count of the enemy, Rufina wants help with her plan, Jennings wants a guide and Fenwick wants Flotsam out of the way. The player sounds the alarm, runs the boats with the divers, or brokers the surrender. Every road leads to Nassau, but on different boats and with different friends.

### Act III: The Wandering Isle (Nassau and the Matanilla shoals, February–March 1716)

**7. The Flying Gang** (`dh-07-nassau`). Nassau: tents, a ruined fort and sloops heeled over on the careening beach like stranded whales. Lalo finds a bottle in Inés's hand: she's alive, a captive aboard the *Restitution*, and she's been sailing Wraxall in circles by lying about the sun. Wraxall makes his case in the punch house. To win a ship, Flotsam earns the crew's votes in the Knot Yard, on the hull and in a dispute over shares. Flotsam also sets Inés's watch to Havana time by Nassau's noon. At the bonfire vote the player signs the articles, hires the sloop or turns to Hornigold.

**8. Dead Reckoning** (`dh-08-reckoning`). At sea, Flotsam navigates with real methods: a noon sight for latitude, the watch against local noon for longitude, the log-line, the traverse board and the lead. A squall forces them to reef the sail. Then the *Restitution* is sighted, Inés signals, and Flotsam boards her, timing the swing across for the moment the two hulls roll together. In the melee Flotsam can throw old Oakes a line or seize Wraxall's chart case. Wraxall escapes into the squall, and Inés is free. Then she says the hardest thing: "Eight days. It's been seven months. She's stopped. The proof is broken."

**9. The Wandering Isle** (`dh-09-isle`). A low cay ringed by wrecks, with a half-built timber light on its north rock and a blue hole in its heart. Tamsin Penhallow meets them with a fowling piece and a secret: the Heart has never stopped, because she has wound it every morning for seven months. Five days ago she broke her wrist and hid it in the sea cave below the blue hole the islanders call the Throat. The clock will stop in three days. Flotsam watches the Lusca breathe, dives at slack water and finds the ticking in the dark. That night, from the ridge, Inés times an eclipse of Jupiter's moon Io and checks the clock against it: three minutes and forty-two seconds off in seven months, which is under a degree. Then the wind swings north, and the *Restitution* appears.

**10. The True Light** (`dh-10-light`). A norther, a landing party and an unlit tower. Flotsam climbs the timber light, walks the capstan to haul up the 24-candle chandelier and lights the ring in the wind. Then comes the choice: the true light on the tower, or a false one on a wreck's mast to drive Wraxall onto the reef. Allies arrive according to old debts. On the lantern gallery the player duels Wraxall, and he ends up hanging from the rail. At dawn the casket is opened on the gallery. The last choice is who keeps the hour. Then Flotsam walks the meridian line of light along the beach, home to the Chart Room.

**Unlock rule for the King's Hour:** offered if `dh.saved` is book, or `dh.salmon` is truth, or `dh.raid` is alarm or parley. Any one of them is enough.

## Endings

The final choice comes at dawn on the lantern gallery. Each ending plays a short scene there, then old Lalo's epilogue in the Chart Room, whose furnishings change to match.

**A. Every Ship's Hour.** *"Everyone. Copy her, and send the copies to every port."* Inés, Lalo and Flotsam make drawings of the Swallow, and thirty letters go out to academies, guilds and harbourmasters under every flag. Three are answered and the rest ignored, which Inés says is a good rate. No empire owns the method, and none adopts it. The Chart Room gets a half-built copy of the clock on Lalo's bench and a drawer of replies. Title: **Keeper of the Open Hour**. Cosmetic: *Swallow Brooch*.

**B. The King's Hour.** *"The King. Send her to Havana."* (Needs one Spanish-trust flag.) Salmón's letter and Anselmo's seal carry the casket to the Casa de Contratación, and Inés is named Cosmographer, the title her father held. The method is classified as a state secret. In 1717 the Casa moves to Cádiz and the clock disappears into a sealed archive; history never hears of it. The Chart Room gets a royal commission framed beside an empty cradle. Title: **Pilot of the Casa**. Cosmetic: *Casa Sash*.

**C. The Promised Hour.** *"London. Let the Board pay what it promised."* The clock sails for London in Wraxall's care if he lives, or Oakes's if he doesn't. The prize can't be paid until the Commissioners of Longitude meet, and they don't meet until 1737. Wraxall, if alive, waits twenty years in a rented room with the Swallow ticking on his table, and dies in 1736. The Chart Room gets a letter promising £20,000 "upon the convening of the Board". Title: **Twenty-Thousand-Pound Promise**. Cosmetic: *Unawarded Medal*.

**D. The Drowned Hour.** *"No one. Give her back to the Throat."* The casket goes down into the blue hole at slack water, still ticking. Inés's reaction depends on `dh.promise`. If Flotsam promised, she says "You promised me," and doesn't speak to them until noon, and then only to say the sun is still where it should be. If Flotsam said they'd do what's right, she says "You did. I'd have done otherwise. One of us is wrong." If they hushed her, she says "Perhaps the sea was owed one thing it didn't have to steal." The Chart Room gets a coral-crusted winding key under glass. Title: **Keeper of the Drowned Hour**. Cosmetic: *Lusca's Ring*.

**Epilogue variables** (old Lalo tells them over the Chart Room table):
- **Tito.** If Anselmo's book was saved, he was freed by his own contract in spring 1716. If the divers ran the boats, he signed the *Small Mercy*'s articles and later built boats on Eleuthera. Otherwise Rufina paid his last peso in 1719, and he walked out of Merino's yard without looking back.
- **Fenwick.** If freed, he took the King's pardon in 1718 and piloted ships into Nassau for twenty years, charging them in Latin. If left, he took the pardon and the first ship out, and wrote a letter that began "Whereas". If reported, he sailed north and never forgave Flotsam, "which is a kind of remembering".
- **Wraxall.** If saved, he taught navigation at the Royal Mathematical School at Christ's Hospital and told the charity boys to be exact, and kind. If he fell, the sea kept him or let him go, and no chart says which. Ending C overrides both.
- **Oakes.** If saved, he went home to Whitby and named a coble *Flotsam*.
- **Tamsin.** With the true light, Penhallow Light burned for forty years, and since then the Wandering Isle has had the same longitude on every chart. With the false light, the tower was never finished and the isle went back to wandering.
- **Anselmo.** If his book was saved, the Book of the Drowned reached Seville with every name. If not, he rewrote six hundred names from memory and grieved the rest by number.
- **Daire.** If warned, a letter from Le Havre: "Your sky was correct. My nose was also correct. Let us share the credit."
- **Lalo.** Always becomes the chart-maker. "I drew the Wandering Isle where it is. That is the only kind of map I make now."
- **History** (every ending): "In 1735 a Lincolnshire carpenter named John Harrison finished a sea clock of his own. The world remembers him, and that's as it should be. He built his."

## Originality self-audit

- **Banned sources checked.** Nothing here comes from Pirates of the Caribbean, Sea of Thieves, Assassin's Creed IV: Black Flag, One Piece, Monkey Island or Treasure Island. There's no curse, no undead crew, no ghost ship, no kraken, no magic compass, no sea god. There are no riddle maps, no X-marks-the-spot, no paced-out treasure, no talking skull, no parrot, no one-legged cook, no black spot and no marooned castaway who has moved the treasure. Nobody impersonates a dead man to get to Havana, there's no observatory or ancient device, there's no trading company villain who talks about "good business", and there are no insult duels.
- **Shared ground is history, not anybody's fiction.** Havana, Nassau, the 1715 fleet, Jennings, Hornigold and diving bells all appear in other works because they're real. We use them sparingly. Real people get cameos, and the main cast is original. Blackbeard, Vane, Bellamy and Rogers are kept off stage on purpose. Free-diving with air pockets replaces the diving-bell set piece players may know from elsewhere.
- **The central idea is ours.** The treasure is a longitude clock hidden behind a jewel legend, its rubies are bearings, and it keeps ticking because a lighthouse keeper thinks it's a heart. It rests on real history (the 1704 ruby-bearing patent, the 1714 Act, Scilly in 1707, and the Board that didn't meet until 1737) without borrowing any story's plot.
- **Names checked against well-known fiction.** We renamed early drafts to avoid collisions: a Kell (a fantasy-novel hero), a Morwenna (*Poldark*), a Lázaro Cárdenas (a real president), and a Merrick (a *Sea of Thieves* character).
- **Folklore and history are credited.** The Lusca, the Giraldilla, Antillia and *hurakán* are genuine folklore and etymology, and the codex says where each comes from.
