# The Day Begins at Sunset

*A single-player story campaign for Elsewhere. Florence, 1494 to 1498, in eleven chapters.*

> In Florence the clocks count the hours from sunset: when the twenty-fourth hour strikes, the old day ends and a new one begins. Florence's old day is ending.

## Logline

A traveller steps out of a painter's perspective frame into snowbound Florence and becomes the apprentice of a widowed lens-maker. Over four years of revolution, sermons and bonfires, the apprentice must work out who is staging the city's miracles, before the man behind them uses Florence's last great fire to slip away.

## Tone

- **Wonder first.** Snow on Brunelleschi's dome, a crystal blazing in a snow giant's hand, and a glide out of a burning pyramid on its own heat.
- **A civic thriller underneath.** Factions, a mob, a trial by fire, a city deciding who to believe.
- **Workshop warmth.** Craftspeople who tease each other, argue about gears and hide their fear in jokes.
- **Moral weather, no villains by default.** The friar is sincere, the Medici are human, and the antagonist loves beauty. People are dangerous because each of them is sure.
- **Rating: T.** Peril, fire, crowds turning violent, plague and an execution are all handled offscreen or at a distance. Nobody dies on screen and the player never kills. The strike key cuts ropes, rings bells, breaks ice and knocks torches away.

## Themes

1. **Perspective.** Where you stand decides what you see. This is the lesson of Brunelleschi's vanishing point, and also of every faction in the city. Most puzzles are about standing in the right place.
2. **What deserves to be saved.** The Bonfire of the Vanities burned mirrors, lutes, books and paintings. The player keeps choosing what to save, and what each choice costs someone.
3. **The price of the truth.** Exposing a fake miracle also wounds the honest people who believed in it, and the friar who never asked for it.
4. **Festina lente, "make haste slowly".** This is the player's name and the story's secret. Zeal is what burns things down, whichever side it comes from.
5. **A republic of many voices.** Bells, printing presses and a Great Council of three thousand citizens stand against a single voice, whether it belongs to a prince, a prophet or a showman.

## The player character: Lente

**Who they are.** A traveller from Junction, of any look and any gender; nobody in the story assumes either. The player character has no voiced lines. They speak through dialogue choices, which are written to suit a quick, curious, decent person.

**How they arrive.** In Junction, a new portal is labelled **Florence** with the blurb *"Step through a painter's veil into snowbound Florence, 1494."* Through it, the player tumbles out of the **Veil**, a 6-metre perspective frame strung with a grid of silk threads. It stands in the top-floor optics loft of the **Bottega all'Occhio** ("the workshop at the sign of the Eye"), a lens-maker's tower-house on the south bank of the Arno.

**Why they stay.** Monna Smeralda, the widow who runs the shop, built the Veil to paint Florence "exactly as it is". She did not expect it to open. She keeps it secret: in a city that is starting to burn mirrors, an open window to elsewhere would be called sorcery. To explain the stranger, she tells everyone Lente is her late husband's relation from Flanders, sent to learn the trade.

She names the player at once:

> "Festina lente. Make haste slowly. You came through my window far too fast. Lente, then. It also means *lens*. You will earn the name."

**The Veil is the portal.** The Bottega's `w.portal` back to Junction sits inside the Veil's wooden frame. The player can always leave by it, and in the fiction the Veil only opens for travellers.

**Between chapters.** The Bottega is the campaign's home and hub. Its street door leads to the current chapter, and it changes as the story goes on.
- Masino's stool by the furnace is empty for a year, then occupied again or not.
- A **Gallery of Saved Things** fills with what the player rescued.
- A **Wall of Titles** shows what they have earned.
- A painted **Calendar** replays finished chapters.

## Setting and history

### The four years

### Liberties we take

- **Invented people and places.** The Bottega, Smeralda, Masino, Ottaviano, Cassandra, Bartolo and the Veil are invented, and so are all the "miracles" and the art-smuggling plot. History records omens and rumours, not these tricks.
- **Capponi's bells.** Historically his threat was enough and no bells rang. In our version the player sets up the signal, and **la Vacca strikes once** as the city's answer.
- **The ordeal.** History's own outcome stands (disputes, rain, cancellation). We add a hidden trap under the platform that the rain and the player defeat.
- **The Marzocco.** Our weeping lion is an older stone Marzocco on the Palazzo's speaking platform. Donatello's famous one only reached the piazza in 1812.
- **The Duomo clock.** Paolo Uccello's 24-hour dial (1443) is real, and so are its anticlockwise hand and "Italian hours". For readability, our dial puts hour XXIV at the top. The machinery behind it is our own design.
- **The Paradise machine.** It is based on Vasari's description of Brunelleschi's festival machine for San Felice in Piazza. Its survival in a storeroom is our invention.
- **Lorenzo della Volpaia's planetary clock.** It was made for Lorenzo de' Medici and went to the Palazzo della Signoria in 1510. Our version is rescued during the 1494 sack and taken into the Signoria's keeping at once.
- **Scale.** Florence is built at about half scale, with distances compressed between landmarks.

### Factions

- **The Medici and the *Bigi* ("greys").** The fallen ruling family and its quiet friends. They are patrons of art and philosophy who also ran a republic like a private bank. After 1494 they plot from exile.
- **The *Piagnoni* ("weepers").** Savonarola's followers: merchants, artisans, artists and devout women. They want a purified, just city. Their sincere core includes Landucci and Botticelli's circle.
- **The *Fanciulli*.** The friar's boys, in white robes and carrying red crosses. They go door to door collecting "vanities", singing lauds and policing gambling. Masino joins them.
- **The *Arrabbiati* ("enraged") and the *Compagnacci* ("bad companions").** The aristocratic opponents of the friar, with their street arm of rich young men led by Doffo Spini.
- **The Republic.** The Signoria (eight priors and a Gonfaloniere), the Eight of Ward (police and criminal court) and the new Great Council. Everyone fights over who controls them.
- **The Guilds.** Seven major guilds and fourteen minor ones. The Bottega belongs to the Physicians and Apothecaries guild, as painters did, because pigments came from apothecaries. The guild tolerates a widow running her late husband's shop only grudgingly.
- **Ottaviano's crew, "the Angels".** Riggers who flew as boy-angels on ropes in the old festival machines. Now grown and out of work, they hang lamps, build stages and do whatever Ottaviano pays for. They are a work crew, not a secret society: everyone in the Oltrarno knows them.

## The cast

### Original characters

**1. Lente (the player).** A traveller from Junction and Smeralda's apprentice, known to everyone else as "the Fleming".
- **Arc:** from outsider to the person the city's quarrels pass through. Lente ends the story deciding what Florence is allowed to see.

**2. Monna Smeralda dei Vetri (45).** Lens-maker, clockwork tinkerer and maker of the Veil. She runs the Bottega under her dead husband Bonaccorso's name.
- **Voice:** dry, exact and quick. She thinks in focal lengths and measurements. She uses craft oaths ("Saint Luke's thumbs!") and rarely raises her voice, but when she does, glass rattles.
- **Habits:** she cleans her spectacles when she is lying.
- **Secret:** she ground the great lens the miracle-maker used. She took an anonymous commission because the guild was about to close her shop.
- **Arc:** from detached observer ("I make the glass; others choose what to look at") to participant. In the best ending she shows the whole city how its miracles were made, including her own part.

**3. Masino (12 to 16).** The Bottega's orphan shop-boy.
- **Voice, early:** fast and eager. He asks ten questions at once, quotes the friar's sermons with gusto and gets the Latin wrong.
- **Voice, as a captain of the fanciulli:** grave, gentle and certain.
- **Arc:** from boy to believer to someone who has to decide, after the friar falls, what he still believes. Whether he comes home to the Bottega depends on how honest the player was with him.

**4. Ottaviano Scorci (50).** Master of festival machines (*ingegni*), perspective painter and intarsia artist, "the man who makes walls look like windows".
- **Past:** he built Lorenzo de' Medici's carnival triumphs.
- **Present:** the friar has ended Carnival, so Ottaviano now builds whatever spectacle someone pays for, including miracles.
- **Voice:** warm, theatrical and generous. He talks in aphorisms, cues, lamps and audiences, and hums Lorenzo's carnival song *"Quant'è bella giovinezza"*. He never shouts. His menace is his calm.
- **Arc:** the antagonist, revealed in Chapter 7. He loves beauty sincerely and cannot tell moving people from using them. He can be exposed, let go, ruined, or, in the secret ending, given what he always wanted: one true wonder.

**5. Cassandra del Torchio (24).** A printer who runs her late father's press in a cellar near the Badia.
- **Voice:** blunt, quick and mercantile ("Everything's true at a quattrino a sheet"), with ink to the elbows.
- **Story:** she prints the friar's sermons and the pamphlets against him, and every miracle broadsheet. Some of the broadsheets were paid for *before* the miracle happened.
- **Arc:** from mercenary to conscience. She decides a press should print what is true even when nobody pays. In the endings, she is the one who tells the city.

**6. Bartolo Nuti (63).** Under-keeper of the Medici sculpture garden at San Marco, where he once taught a scowling boy named Michelangelo to sharpen chisels.
- **Voice:** gentle and rambling. He talks about trees and statues, calls Lorenzo "the Magnificent" or just "him", and cries easily.
- **Arc:** loyalty to a dead man makes him a courier for the Medici exiles. He is the red herring of the mystery, framed by Ottaviano. The player's choices decide whether he escapes over the hills, rots in prison, or comes back to repay the player in the siege of San Marco.

### Real historical figures

They are portrayed respectfully and only as far as the record plausibly allows.

**7. Fra Girolamo Savonarola (42–45).** Dominican, prior of San Marco and the most powerful voice in Florence.
- **Voice, in the pulpit:** thunder, repetition and scripture ("Florence, Florence, Florence…").
- **Voice, in private:** short, plain sentences and piercing questions. He is kind to children and hard on himself.
- **Arc:** he never asks for the fake miracles, and is diminished by them. In his tower cell he is honest, frail and unafraid.

**8. Piero de' Medici (22).** Lorenzo's son. Charming, athletic, careless, and out of his depth.
- **Arc:** in Chapter 1 he laughs in the snow; in Chapter 2 he runs.

**9. Michelangelo Buonarroti (18–19).** Sculptor, already brilliant, broken-nosed and proud.
- **Voice:** grumpy, funny and blunt about stone, snow and fools.
- **Arc:** he appears in Chapters 1 and 2. He leaves Florence after Cardiere's dream and is remembered through letters and gossip.

**10. Sandro Botticelli (about 50).** The painter, gentle, melancholy and self-mocking. He is drawn to the friar's message and frightened by its fire.
- **Arc:** in Chapter 5 he shows Lente the *Calumny of Apelles*, a painting about false accusation, and trusts the wrong man with a painting.

**11. Niccolò Machiavelli (29).** Not yet in office.
- **Voice:** dry, ironic and short. He looks at the mechanism rather than the miracle.
- **Arc:** at the ordeal he gives the player the key piece of advice: "Watch who is not watching the fire." He returns in the epilogue.

**12. Luca Landucci (about 60).** Apothecary and diarist, with a shop opposite the half-built Strozzi palace.
- **Voice:** fussy, kind and worried. He counts everything ("three hundred and four, I counted them").
- **Role:** the ordinary citizen's eye. His notebook opens every chapter.
- **Arc:** from devoted follower of the friar to a sadder, wiser man.

**13. Lorenzo della Volpaia (48).** Clockmaker, maker of the Medici planetary clock, and a friend of Leonardo da Vinci, who is away in Milan.
- **Voice:** warm and meticulous. He thinks in wheels and hours, and loves gossip about "Lionardo".
- **Role:** in Chapter 2 he gives the player the nine-stave clue.

**14. Piero Capponi (47).** Soldier and statesman. He speaks rarely and briefly, and tears up a king's treaty in Chapter 3.

**15. Fra Domenico da Pescia (about 45).** Savonarola's closest companion: simple, cheerful and fearless. He volunteers to walk into the fire. His scenes in Chapters 9 and 10 are among the story's most human.

**16. Doffo Spini (late 20s).** Leader of the Compagnacci. Swaggering, contemptuous and loud. He leads the crowd at the ordeal and the attack on San Marco.

**Walk-ons** (a line or two each, or no lines):
- Cardiere the lute player.
- Charles VIII (one line: his "capon" pun).
- Nanni the clock-warden.
- Fra Giuliano Rondinelli, the Franciscan champion.
- Gatto and Biscia, two of Ottaviano's riggers.
- A Venetian merchant.
- Fanciulli, guards, butchers and the crowd.

## The plot

Each chapter file holds the full beats. In brief:

### Act I: The Fall of the Balls (1494)

The Medici arms are red balls, *palle*. In November 1494 Florentines chisel them off the walls.

1. **The Snow Window** (January 1494). Lente falls out of the Veil, carries a crystal across a snowbound city, restarts Uccello's backward clock, and lights Michelangelo's snow giant from the Medici roofs. At home, an anonymous order is waiting, for a lens "as wide as a shield".
2. **The Balls Fall** (November 1494). Michelangelo flees a dream, la Vacca tolls, and Piero flees.
   - In the sacked palace, Lente and della Volpaia recover the seven wheels of Lorenzo's planetary clock.
   - Bartolo begs for help with the Seal of Nero.
   - Della Volpaia finds a nine-stave pinion: "No clockmaker cuts nine. That is festival work."
3. **Trumpets and Bells** (November 1494). Smeralda gives Lente the Wing. The French king enters under Ottaviano's cloud-car. Lente sets a mirror relay across four towers, Capponi tears up the treaty, and la Vacca strikes once. Ottaviano offers work.

### Act II: The Ark (1495–1496)

4. **The Sword over the City** (Lent 1495). A flaming sword appears on the white inner dome during the friar's sermon.
   - The projector holds Smeralda's lens.
   - A rigger escapes over the outside of the dome.
   - Cassandra printed the miracle a day early.
   - The player decides what to do with the evidence.
5. **The Garden of Broken Gods** (Carnival 1496). The stone lion weeps red. A roof chase leads to San Felice, and a ride on Brunelleschi's Paradise machine. Ottaviano points at Bartolo, and the player decides what to do about the old man.

### Act III: The Bonfire (1497)

6. **The Collectors** (February 1497).
   - Lente hides the Veil behind a perspective-painted wall before the fanciulli's visit.
   - At night, in a warehouse, Lente watches originals being swapped for copies, then chooses whether to tell Masino.
   - Ottaviano admires the false wall: "Whose perspective?"
7. **The Pyramid of Vanities** (7 February 1497). In the pyramid's hollow heart, Ottaviano confesses and asks for the Veil. The torches fall. Lente saves one treasure and glides out on the fire's updraft.

### Act IV: The Ordeal (1497–1498)

8. **The Villa of Echoes** (August 1497). Plague summer. Lente finds the cache at Careggi, reads a lantern code, overhears the plan in a loggia where whispers carry, and settles Bartolo's fate.
9. **Trial by Fire** (7 April 1498). Lente disarms Ottaviano's igniters under the ordeal platform while the friars argue above. Machiavelli gives the key advice. The storm breaks, and the player chooses whom to follow.
10. **The Piagnona** (8 April 1498). The siege of San Marco: Masino is in the library, the bell is calling the city to fight, and the player rings it or silences it. The friar gives himself up.
11. **The Day Begins at Sunset** (22–23 May 1498). A last talk in the tower cell, a river chase at sunset, the confrontation at the weir, the ending, and dawn on the Ponte Vecchio.

## The endings

At the weir, Ottaviano stands on oil-soaked crates with a lantern. The player has three options, plus a fourth that only appears when its conditions are met.

**A. The Open Window**
- **Choice:** "The city will see what you did." The evidence score must be at least 2.
- Lente flashes a mirror to Cassandra's press, and by morning the city reads *How the Miracles Were Made*.
- Smeralda confesses her lens in public and demonstrates the sword projection in the Piazza.
- The guild fines her, and the city forgives her. The art goes to the Signoria, where the people can see it.
- Ottaviano is exiled to Venice rather than executed, because Lente asks for mercy and the city is tired of fires.
- **Title:** *Keeper of the Open Window.*

**B. The Painted Wall**
- **Choice:** "Take your boat and go."
- Ottaviano burns the ledger that names Smeralda's lens, and sails.
- The art survives, in other cities, under other names. The city never learns how its miracles were made, and every faction remembers them its own way.
- The Bottega is safe and quiet. Smeralda paints over the false wall and never mentions it again.
- **Title:** *Painter of Walls.*

**C. Ashes on the Arno**
- **Choice:** "Drop the lantern, Ottaviano." Ending A also falls here when the evidence is short.
- There is a struggle, the lantern falls and the barge burns. Lente pulls Ottaviano out of the river, and most of the art is lost.
- At his trial his ledger names Smeralda's lens. The guild closes the Bottega, and Smeralda leaves for Venice.
- The Veil still opens for Lente, onto an empty, shuttered loft.
- **Title:** *The Incorruptible.* In the friar's city, certainty always burns something.

**D. Festina Lente** (secret)
- **Conditions:** `fi.c1.answer` is `honest`, all 24 Leaves have been found, Masino comes home, and `fi.c8.bartolo` is `escaped`.
- **Choice:** the extra option *"Say nothing."*
- Given silence instead of a fight, Ottaviano talks himself out. Smeralda arrives and offers him what no patron ever did: a look through the Veil at a real elsewhere.
- He sets the lantern down. A month later the illusionist and the lens-maker stage, together, a public show of how every miracle was made.
- **Title:** *Festina Lente.* The Bottega's loft becomes a little theatre.

## Rewards (all soulbound)

Story play pays only in things that cannot be traded: titles, cosmetics, codex entries, and one functional item, Smeralda's Wing (a glider).
- **24 Leaves.** Loose pages from Smeralda's notebook, hidden in Chapters 1–8. They unlock cosmetics at 8, 16 and 24, and are a condition for ending D.
- **No Elsewhere coins.** The campaign never pays out the shared currency, because a bot could farm it. BUILD.md has the details.

## Originality self-audit

**Assassin's Creed.** There is no order, creed, brotherhood or ancient artefact, and no genetic-memory frame.
- The player arrives through a painter's perspective frame, a device built on Alberti's real *velo*.
- There are no hidden blades and no hay-cart dives. Rooftop jumps land on tenter frames from the wool trade, awnings and gliders.
- Towers are puzzle spaces and glide launches. Climbing them never reveals the map.
- Real figures appear only as the record allows, and none of them is a mentor in a secret war.

**Da Vinci's Demons.** Leonardo never appears: in 1494–98 he was in Milan, and he is only mentioned. There are no cults and no mystic books.

**Dan Brown's novels.**
- There are no codes hidden in famous paintings and no letter-dial cylinders. Mirror writing is never a cipher.
- There is no Dante death mask, no Vasari corridor (it didn't exist yet), and no secret bloodline or conspiracy behind history.
- The clues are mechanical (gear-tooth counts, rosin dust, rope burns, a nicked florin) and come from crafts the player practises.

**Novels set in Savonarola's Florence.** This includes George Eliot's *Romola* and later works.
- None of their names, characters or plots are used: no scholar's daughter, no treacherous Greek husband, no young woman painter in a merchant's house.
- The protagonist, the lens-maker, the showman, the printer, the gardener and the shop-boy are all new.

**Ready Player One** (the platform's inspiration). Nothing from it is borrowed.

**Real sources.** Everything real is either public history or public-domain material:
- Lorenzo's carnival song;
- Savonarola's Latin *Ecce gladius Domini*;
- Capponi's retort;
- Alberti's window;
- Vasari's account of the Paradise machine;
- Landucci's role as a diarist (his notebook entries are newly written, not quoted).

**Our own invention.**
- **Mechanics:** the nine-stave pinion clue, the Veil, the weeping-lion cistern, the painted false wall, the pyramid with a hollow heart, and the double-rigged fire corridor.
- **Story:** the substitution trade and all four endings.
